\begin{abstract}
A large language model (LLM) can give a wrong answer for two very different internal reasons: the correct answer never became a competitive candidate inside the model, or it briefly became competitive at some intermediate layer and then lost that advantage by the final layer. These two cases produce the same observable error but describe different failures and call for different interventions. We study them together, tracing the correct-answer signal layer by layer, and introduce a calibrated procedure that separates these two failure modes. We construct, for each question, a trajectory of the gold answer's probability, its full-vocabulary rank, and its log-probability margin over a competing token, across three model families (Qwen3-8B, Llama-3.1-8B, Mistral-7B) and three tasks (TriviaQA, HotpotQA, GSM8K). We first show that a common shortcut, checking whether the gold answer ever outranks the final wrong answer, is misleading: its sign-reversal rate (88.2\%) is statistically the same as an endpoint-conditioned null (88.5\%). After correcting for this selection bias and for readout miscalibration, we find that most errors are formation failures and only a small, task-dependent subset (8.9\%, concentrated in knowledge QA) is a preservation failure. The trajectory prefix also predicts later decay with AUC 0.955, and this predictive signal transfers across tasks (0.935) and across model families (0.970 for Qwen to Llama). Ablating the gold-answer direction in late layers flips 40\% to 76\% of correct examples to wrong answers, far more than any token-direction control. We release \texttt{InfoDyn-Bench}, a benchmark of layered information trajectories, measurements, and prediction labels.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

When a language model produces a wrong answer, the final output does not reveal how the failure happened \citep{ji2023survey, huang2023survey}. The correct answer may never have become a competitive candidate within the model, or it may have emerged at an intermediate layer and then been weakened by later computation. Both paths end at the same wrong string, yet they are different failures and likely require different fixes.

\begin{figure}[t]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.95\textwidth]{figures/fig1_concept.png}
\caption{Conceptual overview of Information Dynamics. When an LLM outputs an incorrect answer, internal representations may either fail to form a competitive gold-answer signal (\emph{formation failure}) or build a competitive signal at intermediate layers that decays before the final layer (\emph{preservation failure}). Our calibrated framework traces these dynamics across layers.}
\label{fig:concept}
\end{figure}

We distinguish two internal scenarios, as illustrated in Figure~\ref{fig:concept}. In a \emph{formation failure}, the gold answer never becomes competitive at any layer before the final one. In a \emph{preservation failure}, the gold answer is briefly competitive and then loses its advantage. Section~\ref{sec:def} gives the exact definitions; informally, we judge competitiveness by whether the gold answer enters the top part of the vocabulary distribution and holds a positive margin over a competing token.

Recognizing these two cases is harder than it looks. A natural approach decodes the hidden state at each layer using techniques like logit lens \citep{nostalgebraist2020logitlens} and checks whether the gold answer ever outranks the model's final wrong answer. We find this shortcut unreliable for three reasons. The final wrong answer is chosen at the last layer, so conditioning on a final error makes intermediate sign flips statistically expected. Gold beating one wrong token does not mean it beats most of the vocabulary. And applying the final language-model head to intermediate states can misread the signal due to geometric miscalibration \citep{belrose2023tunedlens, din2023jump}. Because of these issues, a temporary logit advantage by itself tells us nothing about whether the model ever "knew" the answer.

We address these problems with a calibrated, trajectory-based procedure. For each example we build an information trajectory made of three numbers at every layer: the gold answer's log-probability, its rank in the full vocabulary, and its log-probability margin over a competing token. We pair these measurements with two corrections. An endpoint-conditioned null tells us how many sign flips to expect by chance given that the output is wrong. A tuned lens \citep{belrose2023tunedlens}, a per-layer linear translator, recalibrates the readout so that intermediate states are decoded fairly. Together these let us tell formation failures from preservation failures.

Our results are four results. First, the naive sign-reversal rate (88.2\%) is indistinguishable from the endpoint null (88.5\%), so that shortcut cannot establish a previously competitive gold signal. Second, under the calibrated criterion only 8.9\% of errors show a competitive-then-decaying gold signal. Third, this preservation-failure regime is task-dependent: it appears in knowledge QA (TriviaQA, HotpotQA) and is absent for GSM8K's final-answer token. Fourth, trajectory prefixes predict later decay (AUC 0.955) and the prediction transfers across tasks and model families.

We make four contributions. We diagnose a selection bias in a widely used shortcut for reading intermediate states, showing that pairwise sign reversals cannot establish a previously competitive gold signal. We define an operational criterion that separates formation failures from preservation failures and apply it to an error taxonomy that is task-dependent. We show that trajectory history predicts future decay better than a single-layer snapshot, and that this predictive signal transfers across tasks and model families. Finally, we show that the gold-answer direction is behaviorally necessary for correct outputs, and we release a benchmark of layered trajectories and labels.

\section{Related Work}

\subsection{Decoding Intermediate Representations}
Logit lens \citep{nostalgebraist2020logitlens} and tuned lens \citep{belrose2023tunedlens} decode intermediate hidden states back into the vocabulary space. Probing studies ask whether linguistic or factual information is decodable at specific layers using diagnostic classifiers \citep{alain2016understanding, tenney2019bert, hewitt2019structural, belinkov2022probing}. Short-circuiting and early exit approaches study early prediction readiness \citep{schuster2022confident, din2023jump}. These works ask whether a signal is \emph{present} at a given layer. We instead study how a correct-answer signal \emph{evolves dynamically across layers} and systematically quantify readout and selection biases.

\subsection{Knowledge and Factual Recall}
Work on factual knowledge localization, knowledge neurons, and model editing asks where factual associations are stored in feed-forward layers \citep{geva2021transformer, dai2022knowledge, meng2022rome, meng2023memit, geva2023dissecting}. Known-versus-unknown detection and latent knowledge discovery investigate whether internal representations track model correctness \citep{kadavath2022language, burns2022discovering, azaria2023internal, yin2023do, li2024inference}. We do not aim to edit or locate static facts; instead, we track how the correct-answer signal dynamically rises or decays along the network's depth dimension.

\subsection{Hallucination and Error Prediction}
Uncertainty estimation, hidden-state failure prediction, and hallucination detection analyze intermediate representations to identify potential errors \citep{schuster2022confident, kuhn2023semantic, azaria2023internal, manakul2023selfcheckgpt}. We target a distinct task: predicting whether a competitive correct-answer signal will undergo subsequent decay, evaluating whether trajectory dynamics add predictive value over single-layer snapshot features.

\subsection{Mechanistic Interpretability and Causal Intervention}
Transformer circuit analysis, direct logit attribution, and activation patching decompose network computations into functional components \citep{elhage2021mathematical, olsson2022incontext, wang2022interpretability, nanda2023progress}. Representation engineering and activation steering modify hidden states to alter output behavior \citep{turner2023activation, rimsky2023steering, zou2023representation}. We perform component-level attribution on information trajectories to isolate late-layer components associated with correct-signal decay.

Prior work has not, to our knowledge, unified trajectory-based answer dynamics, endpoint-selection bias, vocabulary-wide competitiveness, calibrated readout, and cross-model decay prediction within a single framework.

\section{Problem Definition}
\label{sec:def}

We fix notation here so that every symbol is defined once.

\subsection{Setup}
Let $x$ be a question, $y^\star$ the gold-answer token, and $\hat{y}$ the token the model actually generates. Let $L$ be the number of layers. At layer $\ell$ ($1 \le \ell \le L$), let $h_\ell(x) \in \mathbb{R}^d$ be the hidden representation at the target position, where $d$ is the hidden dimension. Let $W_U \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times |V|}$ denote the unembedding matrix, where $|V|$ is the vocabulary size, and let $w_y \in \mathbb{R}^d$ be the unembedding vector corresponding to token $y$. A lens $g_\ell$ maps the representation to a logit vector, which we turn into a distribution over the vocabulary by a softmax:
\begin{equation}
p_\ell(y \mid x) = \operatorname{softmax}(g_\ell(h_\ell(x)))_y .
\end{equation}
We use two lenses. The raw logit lens \citep{nostalgebraist2020logitlens} sets $g_\ell$ to the final language-model head, applying the final layer norm and unembedding matrix $W_U$. The tuned lens \citep{belrose2023tunedlens} adds a per-layer affine map that is trained so that the decoded logits approximate the final-layer logits.

We define the \emph{gold rank} $r_\ell(y^\star)$ as the number of tokens whose probability at layer $\ell$ exceeds that of the gold answer, plus one. Rank 1 is the top token:
\begin{equation}
r_\ell(y^\star) = 1 + \sum_{y \neq y^\star} \mathbb{1}\big[ p_\ell(y) > p_\ell(y^\star) \big] .
\end{equation}

\subsection{Two Competitive Margins}
We use two related margins in different analyses. The first compares the gold answer with the generated answer $\hat{y}$:
\begin{equation}
\mathrm{CIS}^{\mathrm{gen}}_\ell = \log p_\ell(y^\star) - \log p_\ell(\hat{y}), \qquad \hat{y} \neq y^\star .
\end{equation}
We use this margin for error examples and for the endpoint-bias analysis. The second compares the gold answer with the strongest competing token $y^-$, defined as the most probable token other than the gold answer at the final layer:
\begin{equation}
y^- = \arg\max_{y \neq y^\star} p_L(y), \qquad
\mathrm{CIS}^{\mathrm{comp}}_\ell = \log p_\ell(y^\star) - \log p_\ell(y^-) .
\end{equation}
We use this margin whenever we compare correct and incorrect examples. Table~\ref{tab:metric} states which margin each analysis uses, so the measurement object is never ambiguous.

\subsection{Information Trajectory}
The information trajectory of an example is the per-layer collection of the gold log-probability, the gold rank, and the competitive margin:
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{T}(x) = \big\{ \log p_\ell(y^\star),\; r_\ell(y^\star),\; \mathrm{CIS}^{\mathrm{comp}}_\ell \big\}_{\ell=1}^{L} .
\end{equation}
From this trajectory we derive three quantities. The \emph{peak rank} is the smallest rank over all layers except the final one:
\begin{equation}
r_{\mathrm{peak}} = \min_{1 \le \ell < L} r_\ell(y^\star) .
\end{equation}
The \emph{peak-to-final decay} $D$ is the drop from the largest intermediate margin to the final margin:
\begin{equation}
D = \max_{1 \le \ell < L} \mathrm{CIS}^{\mathrm{comp}}_\ell \;-\; \mathrm{CIS}^{\mathrm{comp}}_L .
\end{equation}
The \emph{competitive decay} label marks an example whose gold answer is competitive at some intermediate layer but negative at the final layer:
\begin{equation}
C(x) = \mathbb{1}\Big[ \exists\, \ell < L : r_\ell(y^\star) \le k \;\land\; \mathrm{CIS}^{\mathrm{comp}}_\ell > 0 \Big] \cdot \mathbb{1}\Big[ \mathrm{CIS}^{\mathrm{comp}}_L < 0 \Big] .
\end{equation}
We use $k=5$ as the main competitiveness threshold and report the sensitivity over $k \in \{1,3,5,10,20\}$.

\subsection{Error Taxonomy}
We define four operational categories under our measurement framework. They describe what the measurement sees, not every conceivable error mechanism. A \emph{formation failure} is an incorrect example whose gold answer never meets the competitiveness criterion at any intermediate layer. A \emph{preservation failure} is an incorrect example whose gold answer meets the criterion but loses its advantage by the final layer. A \emph{successful preservation} is a correct example whose signal forms and is maintained. The remaining category, \emph{unresolved}, covers examples where tracking only the final-answer token is not enough to describe the error, such as multi-step reasoning.

\section{Experimental Setup}

\subsection{Models and Tasks}
We evaluate three open-weight instruction-tuned models: Qwen3-8B \citep{qwen3}, Llama-3.1-8B \citep{dubey2024llama}, and Mistral-7B-Instruct-v0.3 \citep{jiang2023mistral}. We use three tasks: TriviaQA \citep{joshi2017triviaqa} for fact QA, HotpotQA \citep{yang2018hotpotqa} for multi-hop QA, and GSM8K \citep{cobbe2021gsm8k} for math reasoning. We decode greedily with the prompt "Answer with just the answer."

\subsection{Answer Extraction}
We judge correctness by exact substring matching after lower-casing. For numeric answers we strip commas and percent signs and compare numerically with a small tolerance. For TriviaQA, where several aliases are valid, a match against any alias counts as correct.

\subsection{Lens Construction}
The raw logit lens applies the final language-model head directly. For the tuned lens \citep{belrose2023tunedlens} we fit, for each layer, an affine map $(\mathbf{A}_\ell, \mathbf{b}_\ell)$ so that $\mathbf{A}_\ell h_\ell + \mathbf{b}_\ell$, decoded through the final norm and head, approximates the final-layer logits. We train the translators on a held-out subset that is separate from the prediction split.

\subsection{Statistical Testing}
We report every proportion with a bootstrap 95\% confidence interval, and we report per-model and per-task numbers alongside aggregates.

\section{Results}

We first correct the measurement, then present what we find.

\subsection{A Naive Sign Reversal Does Not Show a Competitive Signal}

Our first result concerns the common shortcut of checking whether the gold answer ever outranks the final wrong answer. To see whether such a sign flip carries information, we compared the observed flip rate with an endpoint-conditioned null. The null keeps the final margin negative but shuffles the intermediate layers, so it shows how many flips to expect purely from the constraint that the output is wrong.

The observed sign-reversal rate is 88.2\%. The null rate is 88.5\%. The two are statistically the same (Figure~\ref{fig:bias}). The conclusion is that pairwise sign reversal does not show that a gold signal was ever competitive. Conditioning on a wrong output makes these flips nearly inevitable.

This result matters beyond our setting. Any layer at which the gold log-probability happens to exceed the final wrong answer is not, by itself, evidence that the model "knew" the answer. We therefore drop pairwise crossings and use the rank-and-margin criterion from Section~\ref{sec:def}.

\subsection{Competitive Decay Is the Minority}

To evaluate how frequently a competitive gold signal actually decays in practice, we applied our rank-and-margin criterion to error examples under both uncalibrated and calibrated readouts. On the Qwen analysis subset, the raw logit lens identifies a competitive decay rate of 5.2\%, whereas the tuned lens yields 8.9\% (bootstrap 95\% CI: [6.4\%, 11.5\%]). Readout calibration slightly increases the detected rate because the tuned lens recovers a more accurate gold rank at intermediate layers. We draw two conclusions: preservation failure represents a small minority of errors rather than the primary failure mode, and this phenomenon is a genuine internal trajectory behavior rather than an artifact of uncalibrated projection.

\subsection{The Decay Rate Is Robust to the Competitiveness Threshold}

The competitive-decay label depends on a rank threshold $k$ that defines competitiveness, so we checked how sensitive the rate is to $k$. We recomputed the joint criterion over $k \in \{1,5,10,50\}$ on the Qwen subset, holding the margin condition fixed. The rate rises monotonically from 4.8\% at $k=1$ to 8.5\% at $k=5$, 10.1\% at $k=10$, and 16.9\% at $k=50$. The conclusion that preservation failure is a minority of errors holds at every threshold, and the rate grows only slowly as we relax what counts as competitive. We report $k=5$ as the main setting and note the trend so the reader can judge the robustness of the taxonomy.

\subsection{Raw and Tuned Lenses Agree in Direction but Differ in Magnitude}

The raw lens inflates the intermediate peak margin by roughly a factor of two, yet the ordering between correct and incorrect examples is the same under both lenses. The tuned lens recovers a better gold rank, which is why the competitive-decay rate does not collapse under calibration. The combined picture, lower absolute margins but a stable competitive-decay rate, resolves the tension between readout bias and a real preservation-failure regime.

\section{Can Early Trajectories Predict Later Signal Decay?}

We asked whether the first half of the trajectory predicts whether the signal will decay in the second half. We define a binary decay label $y^{\mathrm{decay}} = \mathbb{1}[\text{signal decays in the second half}]$ and train a linear classifier on features computed only from the first half.

We compared the full-trajectory model against snapshot baselines that use only a single layer or a simple summary, such as the current margin, the current rank, the margin plus slope, the last few layers, the running maximum, and linear extrapolation. The point of these baselines is to test whether history adds anything beyond the current state.

The full-trajectory model reaches AUC 0.955 within setting, well above the current-margin-only baseline of 0.690. The predictive structure transfers across tasks, giving 0.935 from TriviaQA to HotpotQA. Figure~\ref{fig:prediction}a summarizes these comparisons.

\section{Behavioral Intervention and Component Analysis}

\subsection{Gold-Direction Ablation}

We asked whether the correct-answer signal is behaviorally necessary, meaning whether removing it changes the output. We removed the component of the hidden state that lies along the gold token's unembedding direction $w_{y^\star}$ (defined in Section~\ref{sec:def}) in the last three layers:
\begin{equation}
h'_\ell = h_\ell - \operatorname{proj}_{w_{y^\star}}(h_\ell) .
\end{equation}
Here $\operatorname{proj}_{w_{y^\star}}(h_\ell)$ is the projection of $h_\ell$ onto $w_{y^\star}$, so the operation deletes the part of the representation that pushes toward the gold token. We applied this to examples that were originally correct and measured how many flipped to wrong.

The ablation flips correct examples to wrong at high rates in all three models, as Table~\ref{tab:ablation} shows.

\begin{table}[t]
\centering
\caption{Behavioral necessity: ablating the gold direction flips correct examples to wrong, while a random-token control flips far fewer.}
\label{tab:ablation}
\begin{tabular}{lcccc}
\toprule
\textbf{Model} & \textbf{Baseline correct} & \textbf{Gold flip} & \textbf{Random token flip} \\
\midrule
Qwen3-8B & 50/50 & 38/50 (76\%) & 0/50 (0\%) \\
Llama-3.1-8B & 30/30 & 22/30 (73\%) & 0/30 (0\%) \\
Mistral-7B & 30/30 & 12/30 (40\%) & 0/30 (0\%) \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{table}

The gold flip rates are 76\%, 73\%, and 40\% for Qwen, Llama, and Mistral. The random-token control flips 0\% in every model.

\subsection{Controls}

To check that the effect is not just any direction change, we ablated other directions on Qwen3-8B. A random token's unembedding direction flips 0\%, the second-best candidate direction flips 13\%, and a gold-competitor contrast direction flips 33\%. The gold direction gives the highest flip rate.

One caveat is that our strongest non-gold control matches the intervention family but only partly matches the immediate drop in the gold log-probability, because the gold unembedding direction is the main source of that log-probability. We therefore read the result as establishing the behavioral importance of late-layer components aligned with the gold direction, without claiming a fully isolated directional specificity. Figure~\ref{fig:intervention}a shows the flip rates.

\subsection{Direct Logit Attribution}

We located which component contributes most to the margin at the output. We used direct logit attribution \citep{elhage2021mathematical, geva2021transformer}, which attributes the margin to each layer's MLP output vector $m_\ell(x) \in \mathbb{R}^d$ through the contrast direction $w_{y^\star} - w_{y^-}$:
\begin{equation}
\Delta \mathrm{CIS}^{\mathrm{MLP}}_\ell = (w_{y^\star} - w_{y^-})^\top m_\ell(x) .
\end{equation}
Here $y^-$ is the strongest competing token defined in Section~\ref{sec:def}. The quantity measures how much the MLP output at layer $\ell$ pushes the margin toward or away from the gold answer.

The final-layer MLP (layer 35 for Qwen3-8B) has the largest negative direct attribution, with a value of about $-4.55$. We report this as an attribution, not as a proven cause. Confirming a causal role for this component needs activation patching, which we leave to future work. Figure~\ref{fig:intervention}b shows the per-layer attribution.

\section{InfoDyn-Bench}

We release \texttt{InfoDyn-Bench}, a benchmark of layered information trajectories. It stores, for each example, the per-layer gold probability and rank, the margin trajectory, the raw and tuned-lens outputs, the correctness label, the competitive-decay label, and the prediction split, across three models and three tasks. The benchmark supports measurement evaluation, decay prediction, cross-task and cross-model transfer, and intervention-candidate selection. We include a dataset card with the storage format, licenses, and reproducibility scripts.

\section{Discussion and Limitations}

\paragraph{What does "the model knows" mean?}
Competitive decodability is an operational sign of output readiness, not a complete definition of knowledge. We do not equate a top-5 rank with knowledge.

\paragraph{Different failures call for different interventions.}
Formation failures may need retrieval, reasoning correction, or knowledge editing \citep{meng2022rome}. Preservation failures may suit late-layer steering \citep{turner2023activation, zou2023representation}. Reasoning-state failures need tracking intermediate variables.

\paragraph{Why do trajectories predict?}
We conjecture that the slope reflects signal stability, the early peak reflects formation speed, and the fluctuation reflects the competitive state. History therefore separates a transient fluctuation from a stable formation better than a single snapshot.

\paragraph{Limitations.}
The margin is an operational proxy, not knowledge itself. Tracking only the final-answer token does not fully describe multi-step reasoning, as in GSM8K. Multi-token answers remain harder to measure. The tuned lens may itself carry probe bias. The competitive-decay taxonomy depends on a competitiveness threshold, although we show the rate changes slowly with that threshold. The gold-direction ablation shows that the representation aligned with the gold answer is behaviorally important, but it does not prove that an information-preservation mechanism is causally responsible; our non-gold controls only partly match the intervention magnitude. Direct logit attribution is not a complete causal mechanism. And our study covers three model families in the 7 to 8 billion parameter range, so we do not claim generality to much larger or reasoning-specialized models.

\section{Conclusion}

We showed that the same final error can come from different internal dynamics: a correct-answer signal that never forms, or one that forms and then decays. Naive pairwise logit crossings overstate the second case because of endpoint selection and readout biases. After calibration, a small and task-dependent preservation-failure regime emerges, concentrated in knowledge QA. Calibrated information trajectories identify this regime and give transferable signals for predicting future decay across tasks and model families. We release \texttt{InfoDyn-Bench} to support further study of process-level error diagnosis in language models.
